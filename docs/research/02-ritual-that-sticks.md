## Executive Summary

This research synthesizes evidence from cognitive labor theory, couples research, habit formation, business scorecards, and app retention to inform design of a shared weekly planning ritual that reduces mental load and maintains engagement beyond the critical 2–3 week dropout window.

**Key findings:**
- Cognitive labor (anticipating, monitoring) is the most taxing and invisible part of household management
- Weekly check-ins work when they're short (15–30 min), same time/place, and focus on solving vs. status
- Habits take a median 59–66 days to automate; missing one week hurts, missing one day does not
- Productivity apps lose 75% of users by day 30; strong performers retain 10–18%
- Making invisible work visible through named actions reduces conflict and increases follow-through
- SMART goals can backfire for beginners; "whose move next" may work better than rigid metrics

## 1. Cognitive Labor and Mental Load

### 1.1 The Four Phases (Strong Evidence)

Allison Daminger's 2019 study of 35 couples (70 interviews) identified cognitive labor as distinct from physical chores, comprising four phases:

1. **Anticipation:** Noticing needs before they're urgent
2. **Identification:** Working out options
3. **Decision-making:** Choosing between options
4. **Monitoring:** Checking outcomes and adjusting

**Key finding:** Women carried the heavier cognitive load in 26 of the 32 couples analyzed (81%). Women disproportionately handled anticipation and monitoring work, while decision-making was more equally shared. These hidden phases are the most taxing because they're invisible to partners and run continuously in the background.

**Source:** Daminger, A. (2019). The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor. *American Sociological Review*, 84(4), 609–633. https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122419859007

**Confidence:** High. Qualitative study with 35 couples (70 interviews), highly cited (572 citations as of 2026), peer-reviewed top journal.

### 1.2 Mental Load and Health Outcomes (Strong Evidence)

A 2024 USC study of 322 mothers using the Fair Play framework found:
- Mothers reported 73% (72.57%) of cognitive household labor vs. 27% (27.43%) for partners
- Physical labor split was 64% (63.64%) vs. 36% (less skewed than cognitive)
- Greater cognitive burden associated with higher depression, stress, burnout, poorer mental health, and lower relationship quality

**Source:** Aviv, E., Waizman, Y., Kim, E., Liu, J., Rodsky, E., & Saxbe, D. (2024). Cognitive household labor: gender disparities and consequences for maternal mental health and wellbeing. *Archives of Women's Mental Health*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-024-01490-w

**Confidence:** High. N=322, validated measures, peer-reviewed.

### 1.3 Fair Play Intervention Results (Low-Medium Confidence)

An 8-week Fair Play online intervention (two cohorts) found:
- High attrition: Only 33% (47 of 142) completed the intervention
- Cohort 1 participants who completed more modules showed more equitable division of cognitive and physical labor at follow-up
- Cohort 2 showed no significant change (small sample)
- Among those whose division of labor improved, lower stress and depression and better relationship quality observed

**Source:** USC Public Exchange (2024). The Fair Play Method: Final Report. https://public-exchange.org/usc/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2025/09/PX_FairPlay-Final-Report_Dec2024-2.pdf

**Confidence:** Low-Medium. Very high attrition, no control group for follow-up, observational correlations only. Effect direction promising but causality uncertain.

### 1.4 Design Implications

✓ **Make anticipation and monitoring visible.** The page should explicitly name who owns "noticing when X needs doing" and "checking it happened," not just who executes.

✓ **Assign complete ownership, not tasks.** One person owns "meals" (plan, shop, cook, or arrange) rather than splitting subtasks that create coordination load.

✓ **Show both partners' cognitive work.** Visibility reduces resentment; invisibility breeds conflict.

## 2. Couples' Weekly Check-Ins

### 2.1 Gottman State of the Union (Indirect Evidence)

The Gottman State of the Union meeting is a structured weekly check-in covering:
- Appreciation for the past week
- Concerns or needs
- Plans for the coming week

**Evidence base:** No isolated RCT of the meeting itself. It's part of the broader Gottman Method. A 2024 observational study (N=490 intervention, 242 control; propensity score matching) of the 12-hour Seven Principles program found small overall improvement in dyadic adjustment (d = 0.23), with larger effects for participants starting with clinical-level distress (d = 0.62). Effects sustained at 6 months.

**Sources:**
- Gottman Institute (2024). How to Have a State of the Union Meeting. https://www.gottman.com/blog/how-to-have-a-state-of-the-union-meeting/
- Zahl-Olsen, R., Thuen, F., & Bertelsen, T. B. (2024). The effectiveness of the in-person and online Gottman Seven Principles program. *Journal of Marital and Family Therapy*, 50(4), 882-898. https://doi.org/10.1111/jmft.12726

**Confidence:** Medium. No direct test of the weekly meeting alone. Seven Principles program shows modest effects overall; larger for distressed couples. Not an RCT (observational with propensity matching).

### 2.2 Frequency and Duration

**Weekly is optimal.** Consistent weekly rhythm prevents drift. Less frequent risks buildup; more frequent risks compliance fatigue.

**15–30 minutes is the sweet spot** for routine maintenance. Longer sessions needed only when recovering from missed weeks or addressing crisis.

**Sources:**
- Gottman Institute guidance (above)
- Extension literature on family meetings: 15-minute meetings work; participants report it as "most important family activity" in retrospect. https://fonddulac.extension.wisc.edu/files/2011/10/Family-Meeting-handout.pdf

**Confidence:** Medium. Practitioner consensus and qualitative retrospective reports, not controlled experiments.

### 2.3 Design Implications

✓ **Same time, same day, same place each week.** Implementation intentions work (see section 4).

✓ **Start with appreciation.** Lowers defensiveness before difficult topics.

✓ **Short and structured.** Keep to 30 minutes. If it routinely runs long, the page is trying to do too much.

## 3. Family Meetings and Shared Rituals

### 3.1 Family Meeting Evidence (Medium Evidence)

Research on structured family meetings and shared mealtimes:
- Families eating together ≥3x/week: children show healthier eating, higher self-esteem, better academic outcomes, lower substance use
- Weekly family meetings: improved cooperation, conflict resolution, reduced last-minute crises

**Sources:**
- Hammons & Fiese (2011). Is frequency of shared family meals related to nutritional health? *Pediatrics*. Meta-analysis of 182,836 children. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2010-1440
- Harrison et al. (2015). Systematic review of family meal frequency and psychosocial outcomes in youth. *Canadian Journal of Dietetic Practice and Research*. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4325878/

**Confidence:** Medium-High for family meals (strong observational data, causal direction uncertain). Medium for family meetings (mostly guidance literature, limited experimental work).

### 3.2 Design Implications

✓ **Ritualize it.** Consistent timing builds expectation and reduces activation energy.

✓ **Include all household members when age-appropriate.** Shared ownership matters.

✓ **Keep it collaborative, not directive.** Parents who lecture lose engagement; consensus-based problem-solving works better.

## 4. Habit Formation

### 4.1 How Long Does It Take? (Strong Evidence)

Philippa Lally et al. (2010) tracked 96 participants forming daily habits over 84 days:
- **Median time to automaticity: 66 days** (mean of successful participants)
- **Range: 18 to 254 days** depending on person and behavior
- Simple behaviors faster than complex ones
- Automaticity follows an asymptotic curve: fastest gains in first few weeks, then plateau

**Source:** Lally, P., et al. (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 40(6), 998–1009. https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674

**Confidence:** High. Real-world RCT, objective daily tracking. Replicated by Keller et al. (2021) with median 59 days.

### 4.2 Missing a Day vs. Missing a Week (Strong Evidence)

From the same Lally study:
- **Missing one day:** Automaticity dipped 0.29 points (small), recovered next day with no long-term cost
- **Missing a week:** Significantly reduced likelihood of future performance and habit acquisition

**Implication:** One missed Sunday is survivable. Two or three in a row is a danger zone.

**Source:** Same as 4.1.

**Confidence:** High.

### 4.3 Implementation Intentions (Strong Evidence)

Peter Gollwitzer's if-then planning:
- **Effect size: d = 0.27 to 0.66** depending on outcome type (behavior, cognition, affect)
- Enhanced when plans are: (a) in if-then format, (b) rehearsed, (c) supported by high motivation
- Specifying when and where increases follow-through vs. abstract goal statements

**Source:** Sheeran, P., Listrom, O., & Gollwitzer, P. M. (2025). The when and how of planning: Meta-analysis of 642 tests of implementation intentions. *European Review of Social Psychology*, 36(1), 162–194. https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563 [Published online 2024, volume 36 issue 1, 2025]

**Confidence:** High. Meta-analysis of 642 tests from 294 reports, multiple populations, rigorous methodology.

### 4.4 Habit Stacking / Anchoring (Medium Evidence)

Linking new behavior to existing routine:
- RCT (N=192): Routine-based cues and time-based cues similarly effective
- Median 59 days to peak automaticity in successful participants
- Key predictor: repeated enactment at the cue, not the type of cue

**Source:** Keller, J., et al. (2021). Habit formation following routine-based versus time-based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial. *British Journal of Health Psychology*. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.12504

**BJ Fogg's Tiny Habits:** Promising but limited direct evidence. One gratitude RCT showed short-term gains vs. inactive control, partly sustained at 1 month, but active control also improved. Large study found mini-habits had small, statistically uncertain effect.

**Confidence:** Medium. Routine-linking works; optimal cue type unclear. Tiny-specific evidence thin.

### 4.5 Design Implications

✓ **Anchor to a reliable weekly routine.** "After Sunday breakfast" is better than "sometime Sunday."

✓ **Plan for the critical 8–10 week window.** Most habit formation happens in first 2 months; dropout risk peaks at weeks 2–4.

✓ **Forgive one miss, not three.** The page should gently prompt return after one skipped week, escalate encouragement after two.

✓ **Rehearse the plan.** First-time users should walk through the ritual before week 1.

## 5. Business Scorecards: EOS L10 Meetings

### 5.1 The L10 Scorecard (Practitioner Evidence)

Entrepreneurial Operating System's Level 10 Meeting uses a **5-minute scorecard review** of 5–15 weekly metrics:
- Each owner reports "on track" or "off track"
- No discussion or explanation during review
- Off-track items drop to Issues List for problem-solving later
- Teams rate meeting 1–10 at end; goal is 8+

**Purpose:** Early-warning system, not status theater. If everything is always green, metrics aren't useful.

**Sources:**
- EOS Worldwide (2024). What is a Level 10 Meeting? https://www.eosworldwide.com/level-10-meeting
- EOS Worldwide (2024). Level 10 Meeting Agenda. https://www.eosworldwide.com/blog/the-level-10-meeting

**Confidence:** Low for household use. No peer-reviewed research; widely adopted in business but context differs. Principle (fast review, flag issues, solve separately) is sound.

### 5.2 Design Implications

✓ **Separate review from problem-solving.** Quick status check (green/red) on key areas, then focus time on what's broken.

✓ **5–15 minutes for the scorecard, not 30.** Superachiever's "three results" should take <5 minutes to mark as moved/not moved.

✓ **Rate the meeting itself.** Feedback loop helps people adjust when it's not working.

## 6. GTD Weekly Review (Weak Evidence)

### 6.1 What We Know (Indirect Evidence)

David Allen's Getting Things Done prescribes a 1–2 hour weekly review to "get clear, current, and creative."

**Research base:** No controlled study of GTD weekly review specifically. Adjacent evidence:
- Weekly planning reduced unfinished tasks and rumination (N=208 field experiment)
- Implementation intentions improve follow-through (section 4.3)
- But GTD's complete ritual—unproven

**Sources:**
- Weekly planning study: Claessens, B., et al. (2024). Field experiment on weekly planning. *Frontiers in Psychology*. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10952538/
- GTD critique: No peer-reviewed study found. https://romankalugin.com/getting-things-done-book-notes/

**Confidence:** Low for GTD specifically. Medium for weekly-planning principle.

### 6.2 Design Implications

✓ **Borrow the principle, not the prescription.** Weekly review of open loops is sensible; 2-hour ritual for family planning is overkill.

### 7.1 Productivity App Benchmarks (Industry Data)

Day-7 and Day-30 retention by category (2026 data):

| Category | Day 7 | Day 30 | Source |
|----------|-------|--------|--------|
| Productivity (median) | 15% | 8% | AppsFlyer, UXCam |
| Productivity (strong) | 22–28% | 12–18% | Multiple sources |
| Social | 25–30% | 15–20% | Industry aggregates |
| All apps (median) | 11% | 5% | AppsFlyer 2025 report |

**Interpretation:**
- **75% dropout by day 7** is normal for productivity apps
- **90% dropout by day 30** is baseline
- Strong performers double these rates but still lose most users

**Sources:**
- UXCam (2026). Mobile App Retention Benchmarks. https://uxcam.com/blog/mobile-app-retention-benchmarks/
- Appalize (2026). App Retention Rate Benchmarks by Category. https://www.appalize.com/blog/mobile-trends/app-retention-rate-benchmarks-by-category-in-2026

**Confidence:** High for directional benchmarks. Industry datasets (AppsFlyer 28B+ installs). Caveat: "Productivity" is a broad bucket; planning-specific apps may differ.

### 7.2 Critical Windows

**Day 1:** Did onboarding work? Did users experience value?  
**Day 7:** Is this becoming a habit?  
**Day 30:** Does this have durable product-market fit?

**Peak risk:** Days 7–21. If someone clears day 30, retention curve flattens.

### 7.3 Design Implications

✓ **Week 1 must deliver fast, obvious value.** People need a "this helped" moment in the first session.

✓ **Week 2–4: highest-touch onboarding.** Expect resistance; prompt gently.

✓ **Week 4+ celebration.** Make the month milestone visible; people who hit it are much likelier to stay.

## 8. Streaks and Commitment Devices

### 8.1 Commitment Device Evidence (Strong Evidence)

Commitment devices add voluntary cost to failure, leveraging loss aversion:
- **Financial commitment savings for smoking cessation:** Smokers offered CARES (deposit funds for 6 months, forfeit if fail urine test) were 3.4–5.7 percentage points more likely to pass 12-month test than control (effect sustained beyond commitment period)
- Hard commitments (financial, restricted access) work for binary goals; soft commitments (streaks, identity) better for ongoing habits
- Commitment savings accounts show promise but effect sizes vary by design and context

**Sources:**
- Giné, X., Karlan, D., & Zinman, J. (2010). Put your money where your butt is: A commitment contract for smoking cessation. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 2(4), 213-235.
- Bryan, G., Karlan, D., & Nelson, S. (2010). Commitment Devices. *Annual Review of Economics*, 2, 671-698.

**Confidence:** High for smoking cessation RCT (field trial, objective test). Medium for generalization to other domains.

### 8.2 Streaks Specifically (Medium-Low Evidence)

No RCT isolating streak counters, but:
- Identity-based motivation (Tiny Habits) shows promise but thin evidence
- Loss aversion suggests breaking a streak should sting
- Risk: Excessive commitment can cause anxiety and abandonment

**Confidence:** Medium-Low. Plausible mechanism, widely used, but not rigorously tested alone.

### 8.3 Design Implications

✓ **Show streak, but allow one grace miss per month.** Rigid streaks punish life events; forgiving design retains users.

✓ **"Don't break the chain" works for daily habits.** Weekly ritual may need different framing—"4 of 5 weeks" rather than "perfect streak."

✓ **Pair with identity: "You're a weekly planner."** Soft commitment reinforces without rigid penalty.

## 9. Accountability Partners

### 9.1 What Works (Mixed Evidence)

Study of 149 goal-setters:
- **Weekly progress reports to a friend:** significantly higher goal achievement
- **Public commitment to a friend:** better than private commitment
- **Written goals:** better than unwritten

**Source:** Matthews, G. (2015). The Impact of Commitment, Accountability, and Written Goals on Goal Achievement. Dominican University study. https://exa.ai/library/publication/nhd1tmt4j0c

**Confidence:** Medium. Small study, self-reported outcomes, not peer-reviewed journal.

**Couples support:**
- Partner-provided support predicted 25 more minutes of daily activity (N=119 couples, 28-day diary study with accelerometers)
- Joint participation in activities accounted for ~50% of support effect
- But: Partner-assisted weight loss RCT showed no long-term benefit; gamified partner accountability also null

**Sources:**
- Couples support: Berli, C., Bolger, N., Shrout, P. E., Stadler, G., & Scholz, U. (2018). Interpersonal Processes of Couples' Daily Support for Goal Pursuit: The Example of Physical Activity. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 44(3), 332-344. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167217739264
- Partner2Lose RCT: Lewis, M. A., et al. (2024). *BMC Public Health*, 24, 2009. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-024-19464-z

**Confidence:** Medium. Effects exist but are inconsistent. Likely depends on relationship quality and autonomy-supportiveness.

### 9.2 Design Implications

✓ **Built-in accountability is the whole point of a shared page.** Both partners see the same view; invisible work becomes visible.

✓ **Make reporting easy and automatic.** Friction kills compliance.

✓ **Frame as collaboration, not surveillance.** "Our plan" > "your commitment I'm monitoring."

## 10. Measuring "Lighter"

### 10.1 Validated Short Scales for Stress/Mental Load

**PSS-4 (Perceived Stress Scale, 4-item):**
- 4 questions, 0–16 scale
- Cronbach's α ranges from 0.68–0.82 across studies (acceptable to good reliability; most studies ≥0.70)
- Correlates with depression (r=.41–.69 across studies, commonly .55–.69), anxiety (r≈.51–.55), relationship quality
- Used in Fair Play study and hundreds of others

**Sources:**
- Cohen, S., et al. (1983). Original PSS. *Journal of Health and Social Behavior*.
- Multiple validation studies: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1648070/full (2025 cross-language validation)

**Confidence:** High. Well-validated, widely used, brief.

**Sample PSS-4 items:**
1. In the last week, how often have you felt that you were unable to control the important things in your life?
2. In the last week, how often have you felt confident about your ability to handle your personal problems? (reverse-scored)
3. In the last week, how often have you felt that things were going your way? (reverse)
4. In the last week, how often have you felt difficulties were piling up so high that you could not overcome them?

(0 = never, 1 = almost never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = fairly often, 4 = very often)

### 10.2 Design Implications

✓ **Optional monthly PSS-4 check-in.** 4 questions, 30 seconds. Track "things feel lighter" claim.

✓ **Simpler proxy: "This week felt (lighter/same/heavier)."** One-click sentiment may be enough; don't over-instrument.

✓ **Show trend, not raw score.** "Your 'lighter' trend is up 15% this month" is actionable; raw PSS number is not.

## 11. What Makes Goals Measurable?

### 11.1 SMART Goals Under Fire (Strong Evidence, Contested Application)

SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound) is widely taught but:
- **No theory base:** Not derived from goal-setting theory
- **Mixed empirical support:** Meta-analysis of 52 physical activity interventions (N=5,912) found specific goals *not significantly better* than vague/unclear goals (d=0.589 vs. 0.511, difference not significant)
- **Harmful for beginners and complex tasks:** Rigid targets can increase anxiety and reduce exploration in early stages of learning
- **"Open" or "do your best" goals often as effective** as SMART for creative tasks and novices

**Sources:**
- McEwan, D., Harden, S. M., Zumbo, B. D., et al. (2016). The effectiveness of multi-component goal setting interventions for changing physical activity behaviour: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Health Psychology Review*, 10(1), 67-88. https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2015.1104258
- McEwan, D., et al. (2021). The (over)use of SMART goals for physical activity promotion [review paper]. *Psychology & Health*, 36(12), 1520-1534. https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2021.2023608
- Dunn, K., et al. (2022). SMART goals are no more effective for creative performance. *International Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology*. https://exa.ai/library/publication/vs5kp56ryz3

**Confidence:** High for critique. SMART specificity not evidentially superior for all contexts. Low for what *does* work universally (context-dependent).

### 11.2 Design Implications

✓ **"Named move with a person on it" may beat numeric target.** "Lee: draft Q4 budget" is specific and accountable without rigid metric.

✓ **Measurability = "we'll know if it moved."** Binary (done/not done) often clearer than percentage.

✓ **Let users choose their own specificity.** Forcing SMART on resistant users backfires. Offer structure, don't mandate.

✓ **Start vague, get specific as confidence grows.** Week 1: "sort finances." Week 5: "open joint savings, transfer $500."

## Design Rules for Superachiever's Weekly Page

### 12.1 Structure and Duration

1. **Target 20–30 minutes total** (15 min to fill page, 5–10 min to review together)
2. **Fixed time and place:** Sunday evening at kitchen table > "sometime this weekend"
3. **Three sections:**
   - **Quick status:** Where things stand (home, work). 2 minutes.
   - **Named moves:** Next 3–5 actions with a person on each. 10 minutes.
   - **Last week:** What moved, what didn't. 5 minutes.
   - **Three results (optional):** Mark moved/not moved on the 3 goals set week 1. 2 minutes.

**Rationale:** Matches Gottman structure, shorter than EOS L10, longer than scorecard-only. Fits weekly planning evidence.

### 12.2 Making Work Visible

4. **Show both partners' cognitive labor.** "Sam: notice when trash is full" and "Jordan: remember dentist appointments" make anticipation and monitoring explicit.
5. **Assign complete outcomes, not subtasks.** "Alex owns meals this week" (plan, shop, cook, or delegate) rather than splitting shopping/cooking.
6. **Reveal what didn't happen.** Unchosen options and dropped moves stay visible one more week to surface monitoring work.

**Rationale:** Daminger's cognitive labor research; Fair Play findings.

### 12.3 Measurability Without Rigidity

7. **"Whose move next" > numeric KPI for most items.** "Draft budget" is measurable (you did or didn't); "increase savings 15%" may cause analysis paralysis.
8. **Optional quantification.** Let power users add numbers; don't force beginners into SMART straitjacket.
9. **Three results with binary moved/not moved.** "Build emergency fund" → each week mark ✓ or ✗. Clear, low-friction.

**Rationale:** SMART critique; EOS scorecard simplicity; beginner vs. expert needs.

### 12.4 Habit Formation and Onboarding

10. **Week 1: High-touch, fast value.** Guided walkthrough, pre-filled example. They should leave saying "this clarified something."
11. **Week 2–4: Expect and address resistance.** Gentle prompt if they skip week 2. Escalate at week 3 miss. Show that dropping off now is normal but survivable.
12. **Week 4 milestone.** Celebrate first month. Show streak/progress. "You're in the top 20% of users."
13. **Anchor to existing Sunday routine.** "After breakfast" or "after kids' bedtime" > abstract "Sunday."

**Rationale:** Lally habit formation (59–66 days, one miss OK, week miss hurts); app retention (75% gone by day 7); implementation intentions.

### 12.5 When a Week Is Missed

14. **One miss = gentle nudge.** "It happens. Pick up this Sunday." No shame, no reset.
15. **Two misses = stronger prompt + easy on-ramp.** "Restart in 5 minutes: just the 3 next moves."
16. **Three+ misses = treat as new user.** Re-onboard. Something about the ritual isn't working; surface the friction.

**Rationale:** Lally study (single omission no long-term cost, week-long gaps reduce future performance); compassionate design.

### 12.6 Week 1 vs. Week 4 Differences

**Week 1:**
- Show tutorial overlay
- Pre-fill one example move
- Ask for 3 results (goals) at start
- Don't show missed-week recovery UI

**Week 4:**
- Remove scaffolding
- Show 4-week trend ("3 of 4 completed, 12 moves closed")
- Celebrate: "You've built the habit. Keep going."
- Unlock advanced features: monthly PSS-4 check-in, custom sections

**Rationale:** Progressive disclosure; habit-formation window; avoid overwhelming beginners.

## 13. Three Biggest Failure Modes and Fixes

### Failure Mode 1: Invisible Work Stays Invisible

**What happens:** One partner fills the page, the other reviews passively. Anticipation and monitoring remain unshared. Conflict persists.

**Why:** The page defaults to "tasks" rather than "cognitive phases." Filling it becomes another solo chore.

**Fix:**
- Explicitly label "Who notices?" and "Who checks?" for recurring domains
- Prompt both partners to add items: "Sam, add 2 moves. Jordan, add 2 moves."
- Show a balance indicator: "Sam added 8 items this month, Jordan added 2. Discuss?"

**Evidence base:** Daminger (cognitive labor must be named to be shared); Fair Play (visibility reduces resentment).

---

### Failure Mode 2: The Page Becomes Obligation Theater

**What happens:** Partners dutifully fill it each week but experience no relief. It's another status update, not a thinking tool.

**Why:** They're listing tasks, not solving problems. Cognitive labor isn't reduced—just documented.

**Fix:**
- Add a "stuck" flag: "This item is on here 3 weeks in a row. Should we solve it now or drop it?"
- Include a 5-minute "solve one thing" timer. Don't just review—close one open loop before ending.
- Rate the meeting: "Did this help? 1–10." If consistently <7, surface what's broken.

**Evidence base:** EOS L10 (separate review from problem-solving, solve 3–5 issues per meeting); Gottman (check-ins fail when they become rote).

---

### Failure Mode 3: They Stop at Week 2–3

**What happens:** High engagement week 1, completion week 2, skip week 3, never return.

**Why:** The ritual isn't yet automatic (takes 59+ days). Competing priorities win. They don't see the long-term payoff.

**Fix:**
- **Week 2 insurance:** If they don't open the page by Tuesday evening, send one reminder: "Sunday's plan is ready."
- **Week 3 intervention:** If they skip week 2, make week 3 a 5-minute speedrun—just 3 moves, no retrospective. Lower the bar.
- **Week 4 celebration:** Visual milestone. "You've done this 4 weeks. That's top 20%. The next 4 weeks will feel easier."
- **Show the "lighter" trend early.** Even one data point: "Last week you said 'same.' This week 'lighter.' Progress."

**Evidence base:** Lally (median 66 days to automaticity, but fastest gains early—capitalize on them); app retention (day 7–30 is the cliff); commitment devices (small visible wins increase adherence).

## 14. Confidence Levels and Contested Findings

### Strong Evidence (High Confidence)
- Cognitive labor is distinct from physical tasks and disproportionately burdens women
- Habit formation takes 59–66 days median; missing one day OK, missing a week hurts
- Implementation intentions (if-then planning) improve follow-through (d=0.27–0.66)
- Productivity apps lose 75% of users by day 7, 90% by day 30
- PSS-4 is a validated brief stress measure

### Medium Evidence (Proceed with Caution)
- Gottman State of the Union meetings: no isolated RCT, but part of validated method
- Fair Play intervention: promising correlational data, high attrition, needs replication
- Family meetings: strong qualitative support, limited experimental work
- Accountability partners: effects exist but inconsistent; relationship quality matters

### Weak Evidence (Plausible but Unproven)
- GTD weekly review: no controlled study; adjacent evidence supports planning in general
- EOS L10 scorecard: practitioner lore, no peer review, business context
- Streaks as sole motivator: widely used, no RCT isolation
- Tiny Habits: limited direct evidence; one small RCT, unclear effect size

### Contested or Overturned Findings
- **SMART goals are always superior:** FALSE. Meta-analysis shows specific goals not significantly better than vague for many domains; can harm beginners and complex tasks.
- **21 days to form a habit:** FALSE. Urban myth; real median is 59–66 days.
- **Missing one day resets habit formation (William James 1890):** FALSE. Lally study refuted this.

## 16. Ten-Line Summary: Strongest Findings

1. **Cognitive labor (anticipating needs, monitoring outcomes) is distinct from physical tasks, disproportionately burdens women (~73%), and predicts stress, depression, and relationship conflict.** Making it visible reduces load.
2. **Habits take a median 59–66 days to automate.** Missing one day is survivable; missing a week significantly reduces formation likelihood. The critical dropout window is weeks 2–4.
3. **Implementation intentions (if-then planning: "After Sunday breakfast, we do the weekly page") improve follow-through by d=0.27–0.66.** Same time, same place, every week.
4. **Productivity apps lose 75% of users by day 7, 90% by day 30.** Strong performers (top quartile) retain 22–28% at day 7, 12–18% at day 30. Fast, obvious value in week 1 is critical.
5. **Weekly couples check-ins (15–30 min) are part of validated Gottman method.** Start with appreciation, review concerns, plan ahead. Consistency matters more than duration.
6. **Commitment devices (accountability, written goals, progress reports) increase goal achievement.** But accountability works best when collaborative, not surveillance.
7. **SMART goals are not universally superior and can harm beginners or complex tasks.** Vague or "do your best" goals perform equally well in many contexts. "Named move with a person" may beat rigid metrics.
8. **PSS-4 (Perceived Stress Scale, 4 items) is a validated, reliable brief measure** (α=0.70–0.82) to track whether the ritual makes life "lighter." Correlates with depression (r=.55–.59) and relationship quality.
9. **Fair Play intervention (8 weeks, redistribution of cognitive labor) correlated with lower stress and depression, but attrition was high and causality uncertain.** Promising, needs replication.
10. **Three failure modes: (a) invisible work stays invisible → name anticipation/monitoring explicitly; (b) page becomes theater → include problem-solving, not just review; (c) dropout at week 2–3 → gentle nudges, lower bar for week 3, celebrate week 4 milestone.**
